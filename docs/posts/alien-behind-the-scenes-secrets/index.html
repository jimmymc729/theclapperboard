Nobody On The Alien Set Knew What Was About To Happen — And The Cameras Caught It All

From a real 7-foot Nigerian art student squeezing into a rubber suit to an actress who genuinely passed out on camera, here's the behind-the-scenes chaos that turned a low-budget space thriller into a genre-defining nightmare.

1

They Didn't Warn The Cast About The Chestburster — On Purpose

Ridley Scott deliberately kept the actors in the dark about exactly how gory the infamous dinner-table scene would get, because he wanted raw, unscripted terror instead of performed fear. It worked almost too well: Veronica Cartwright had no idea she'd be hit with a jet of fake blood and reportedly passed out on set from the shock.

2

The Xenomorph Started As A Painting In A Book Ridley Scott Almost Didn't Read

Screenwriter Dan O'Bannon handed Scott a copy of H.R. Giger's art book Necronomicon, and two specific lithographs became the literal blueprint for the alien's design. Giger, who had almost no film experience, ended up hand-sculpting the creature himself — reportedly using a real human skull inside its elongated head just to get the anatomy right.

3

The Alien Was Played By A Guy Discovered In A London Pub

Casting directors needed someone impossibly tall and thin to fill Giger's insectoid design, and they found him by accident: Bolaji Badejo, a 6'10" Nigerian graphic design student with zero acting experience. Scott kept him separated from the rest of the cast for most of the shoot specifically so his appearances on camera would feel genuinely alien.

4

A Woman Leading A Sci-Fi Action Movie Was The Twist Nobody Saw Coming

The script was originally written with no specified genders, and producers made a last-minute call to cast a woman as the sole survivor specifically because audiences wouldn't expect it. Sigourney Weaver was a relative unknown with mostly stage experience when she landed Ripley, a role that quietly rewrote what an action lead could look like.

5

This $11 Million Movie Basically Invented A Whole Genre

Alien was made on a modest production budget of roughly $11 million, yet went on to gross over $100 million worldwide and dominate the 1979 box office. Its blend of slow-burn horror and practical creature effects didn't just make money — it set the template that sci-fi horror is still copying almost fifty years later.
