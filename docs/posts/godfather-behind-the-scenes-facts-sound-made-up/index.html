1

That Horse Head Was NOT A Prop — Francis Ford Coppola Just Straight-Up Admitted It

Coppola actually tried a fake prop head first and hated how fake it looked, so he sent his crew to a New Jersey dog food company to find a real horse already slated for slaughter. The rehearsal used the fake head, but on the day they actually filmed, they swapped in the real one packed in dry ice — and John Marley's terrified reaction is exactly as genuine as it looks.

2

Marlon Brando Basically Invented Vito Corleone's Face With Cotton Balls At His Own Audition

Wanting Don Corleone to look like a bulldog, Brando showed up to his screen test with shoe polish in his hair and tissue stuffed in his cheeks. Coppola loved the effect but knew tissue wouldn't survive an actual shoot, so a dentist built him a custom mouthpiece — an uncomfortable device with metal wire and molded resin pieces that gave Vito his iconic jowly look for the whole film.

3

The Cat In Vito's Lap Was A Random Stray Coppola Found Wandering The Lot

That entire iconic opening scene almost got derailed by an animal nobody cast. Coppola spotted a stray cat roaming Paramount's studio lot and, without a word of warning, just placed it in Brando's lap right before rolling camera — and the cat's loud purring ended up drowning out so much of Brando's dialogue that much of it had to be looped in post-production.

4

James Caan Wore Nearly 150 Squibs For Sonny's Death — And Did It For The Girls On Set

The infamous toll booth massacre required an absurd number of pyrotechnic blood packs strapped directly to James Caan's body, with some accounts citing over 400 squibs rigged across Caan, his jacket, the car, and the booth itself. Caan later admitted the real motivation for enduring the dangerous, bruising shoot wasn't dedication to the craft — it was that there were girls watching on set.

5

Real Mob Bosses Actually Negotiated Changes Into The Godfather's Script

Colombo crime family boss Joe Colombo waged a genuine campaign against the production — complete with death threats to producer Al Ruddy and bomb scares at Paramount's parent company — before striking a deal to drop the words 'Mafia' and 'Cosa Nostra' from the screenplay entirely. Ruddy reportedly gave up almost nothing in the trade since the word only appeared once in Mario Puzo's script anyway.
